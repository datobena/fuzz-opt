## Q1. The methodology canon: exactly what do Klees et al. (CCS 2018) and Schloegel et al. (S&P 2024) recommend?

### Takeaway
Klees et al. never prescribe a fixed trial count — they prescribe *a statistical test* (Mann-Whitney U), *an effect size* (Vargha-Delaney Â12), *≥24 h campaigns with over-time plots*, and *ground-truth bugs over "unique crashes"*; they themselves run 30 trials × 24 h. Schloegel et al. turn this into a hard floor: **at least ten trials, at least 24 hours, a permutation/bootstrap test (not just Mann-Whitney), an effect size, and an explicit uncertainty measure** — and they document that the field overwhelmingly ignores it.

### Cited Findings — Schloegel et al., "SoK: Prudent Evaluation Practices for Fuzzing", IEEE S&P 2024 (2024–2026 tier)

- Corpus: **150 papers** analyzed out of **289 candidate** fuzzing papers ("52% random selection") from seven venues — IEEE S&P, USENIX Security, CCS, NDSS, ASE, ESEC/FSE, ICSE — published **2018–2023**. Yearly breakdown in Table I: 2018=9, 2019=20, 2020=24, 2021=15, 2022=29, 2023=19 — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10220)
- **Statistical tests (Sec. III-B6):** "63% (94) conduct no statistical test at all." "Only 37% (55) of the papers run a Mann-Whitney U test to measure statistical significance, which — paired with few trials — risks that it may never reject the hypothesis." — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Effect size:** "only 10% (15) of studies conduct this test" (Vargha–Delaney Â12); "2% (3) rely on other means to specify the effect". **73% (109) of the papers provide no measure of uncertainty.** — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Trials (Fig. 4):** "1, 3, 5, 10, or 20 trials are the most common repetitions chosen." "Overall, 55% (83) of the papers use fewer than 10 trials in at least one experiment (8 papers use a different number of trials throughout their paper)." "15% (22) of the analyzed papers conduct a Mann-Whitney U-test while having five or less trials." — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Duration (Fig. 2):** "56% (84) of the papers run at least one experiment for 24 hours"; "27% (40) of the works use a runtime of less than 23 hours"; "29% (44) use an even higher runtime." — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Recommendation Sec. V-F (Statistical Evaluation), verbatim:** "Any evaluation should be backed by statistical tests. To enable these tests, we recommend running **at least ten trials**. Alternatively, the number of trials can be calculated via an a-priori power analysis to ensure a sufficient sample size leading to statistically significant results. This is particularly important if the fuzzer under consideration only slightly outperforms the state of the art, where n ≫ 10 may be required." — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- Sec. V-F also recommends **permutation tests / bootstrap methods** ("These methods avoid strong assumptions regarding a normal distribution"), and for >2 arms the **bootstrap version of the ANOVA method** followed by a post-hoc test (Tukey for all-pairs, **Dunnett** if only comparisons to a reference method such as the new technique are of interest), because pairwise Mann-Whitney/t-tests hit the **multiple testing problem**. — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Recommendation Sec. V-D (Evaluation Setup):** document "hardware, experiment runtime, number of allocated cores"; **"Choose at least 24 hours"**; run "with uninformed seeds or multiple seed sets"; "all fuzzers should have fair access to all seeds". — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Recommendation Sec. V-E (Metrics):** evaluate "both code coverage and bug-finding capability"; "specify how coverage is collected"; avoid "bitmaps with collisions". — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Their own reproduction protocol** (useful as a citable default): "We use the settings provided by the original papers where sensible, otherwise **we run 10 trials for 24 hours each, restricting each fuzzer to a single core**." (Sec. IV) — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- Artifacts: "74% (214) publish the code of their technique"; "23% (66) of the papers have one or more badges"; "64 are considered available and 63 functional or reusable". Reproduction: "Only 16 out of 57 eligible papers achieved" reproduction results. *(the 74%/214 figure is computed over the 289-paper candidate pool, not the 150 analyzed — flag if quoted)* — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- Case study outcome worth citing on "randomness": for DARWIN they "find in our FuzzBench results no statistical significant difference between DARWIN and DARWIN_RAND", i.e. the claimed evolutionary mechanism was indistinguishable from randomly changing mutation selection. — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)

### Cited Findings — Klees et al., "Evaluating Fuzz Testing", CCS 2018

- Framing (Sec. 1 / Sec. 3): an evaluation requires choosing "(a) a baseline algorithm B ...; (b) **a representative set of target programs to test**; (c) how to measure A's vs. B's performance, ideally as bugs found; (d) ... how seed files are chosen and how long the algorithm should run; and (e) carrying out **multiple runs** for both A and B and **statistically comparing** their performance." — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Trials + test (Sec. 4):** "an evaluation should measure **sufficiently many trials** to sample the overall distribution that represents the fuzzer's performance, using a **statistical test** to determine that A's measured improvement over B is real, rather than due to chance." Klees give **no numeric minimum**; they use **30 runs** in all their own experiments. — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Which test (Sec. 4):** "one should use the **Mann Whitney U-test** to determine the stochastic ranking of A and B, i.e., whether the outcomes of the trials in A's data sample are more likely to be larger than outcomes in B's. Mann Whitney is **nonparametric** in that it makes no assumption about the distribution of a randomized algorithm's performance; by contrast, the standard t-test assumes a normal distribution." Viable alternatives named: "the **permutation test** and **bootstrap**-based tests." — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Effect size (Sec. 4):** "Arcuri and Briand suggest **Vargha and Delaney's Â12 statistics** (which employ elements of the Mann Whitney calculation). **Bootstrap** methods can also be employed here." — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Power warning, with numbers:** on `gif2png`, "after 24 hours AFLFast finds a median of 51 crashes while for AFL it is 39 (a difference of 12). But performing the Mann Whitney test yields a p value of greater than 0.05, suggesting the difference may not be statistically significant, **even on the evidence of thirty 24-hour trials**." For FFmpeg, medians 369.5 (AFLFast) vs 382.5 (AFL) — also insignificant. — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Duration (Sec. 6):** surveyed timeouts "range from 1 hour to days and weeks. Common choices were **24 hours (10 papers)** and **5 or 6 hours (7 papers)** ... **Six papers ran fuzzers for more than one day**." Short timeouts "of less than 5 or 6 hours, as used by 11 papers, may paint a misleading picture." Recommendation: "evaluations should include plots ... that depict **performance over time**. These runs should consider **at least a 24 hour timeout**." — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700) / [ar5iv rendering](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.09700)
- **Rank reversal over time (Sec. 6), the canonical citation for "don't stop early":** with the empty seed, "AFL found no crashes in `gif2png` after 13 hours, while AFLFast had found nearly 40. But after 24 hours AFL had found 39 and AFLFast had found 52." On `nm` with three sampled seeds: "After 6 hours none of the AFL runs found any bugs in `nm`, while the median number of crashes found by AFLFast was 4; Mann Whitney says that this difference is significant. **But at 24 hours, the trend is reversed**: AFL has found 14 crashes and AFLFast only 8. Again, this difference is significant." — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Variance reporting in the surveyed 32 papers (Table 1):** 17/32 make no mention of the number of trials; of the 15 that did run multiple trials, 12 "did not characterize the performance variance" and instead compared the "average" (assumed arithmetic mean); only 3 papers presented confidence intervals. — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700) / [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.09700)
- **Seeds (Sec. 5):** "Most (**27 out of 32**, per Section 2.2) recent [papers used non-empty seeds]". Recommendations: "papers should be specific about how the seeds were collected", "better still to make available the actual seeds used", "the empty seed should be considered, despite its use contravening conventional wisdom", and use "a variety of seeds". **Conflict flag:** the ar5iv-rendered summary reported "30/32 used non-empty seeds"; the extracted PDF text says 27/32. Use 27/32 or re-check Table 1. — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Metric (Sec. 7):** ground truth is preferred over crash-deduplication heuristics. "all 57,142 crashing inputs deemed 'unique' by coverage profiles were addressed by **9 distinct patches**"; stack hashing still showed "an inflation of **66×**" vs ground truth. — [ar5iv rendering of arXiv:1808.09700](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.09700)

### Cited Findings — the third canonical citation nobody skips: Böhme, Szekeres & Metzman, ICSE 2022

- Setup: **10 fuzzers × 24 programs × 23 hours ≈ 13 CPU years**; some analyses used "30 runs of 23 hours, all fuzzers, 11 subjects". — [ICSE22 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE22.pdf)
- **Explicit selection floor (Sec. 4/5):** "We do **not** recommend using **less than 10 benchmark programs** for coverage-based fuzzer evaluation." — [ICSE22 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE22.pdf)
- **Full recommendation (Sec. 7), verbatim fragment:** "at least 10 representative programs" and "**at least 10 (better 20) campaigns of at least 12 (better 24) hours**". Schloegel et al. restate it as: "they recommend to use at least ten representative programs, each tested at least ten times for at least 12 hours (preferably, each value should be doubled)." — [ICSE22 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE22.pdf); [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- Headline finding: "evaluating 10 fuzzers for 23 hours on 24 programs ... a fuzzer that covers more code also finds more bugs" — strong *correlation* between coverage and bugs, but only **moderate agreement** between the coverage-based and the bug-based *ranking*. — [ICSE22 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE22.pdf)
- Time-budget instability: as summarized by Liyanage et al., "Böhme et al. observed that there is only a **moderate agreement between benchmarking results given a 1-hour versus a 23-hour time budget. The best fuzzer after one hour might be the worst fuzzer after one day.**" — [Liyanage et al., ICSE 2023 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE23-effectiveness.pdf)

### Inferences
- With **9–10 trials per arm**, the user is exactly at Schloegel's floor ("at least ten trials") and Böhme et al.'s floor ("at least 10 campaigns"), but **below** the 30 Klees et al. actually ran. Rounding to 10 (not 9) per arm is cheap and makes the claim "we meet the S&P'24 SoK recommendation" unambiguous.
- Klees' `gif2png` example (12-crash median gap, n=30, still p>0.05) is the single best citation for why the user should **pre-register an effect size and, ideally, an a-priori power analysis** rather than only reporting a p-value. Schloegel explicitly offers the power-analysis escape hatch for exactly this case.
- A **survival/log-rank analysis on time-to-bug with right-censoring** is *stronger* than what either canon paper prescribes (both assume a scalar per-trial outcome such as #bugs or coverage). The user should say so explicitly and note that Mann-Whitney on censored times is biased, which is precisely the gap survival analysis fills. Neither Klees nor Schloegel discusses censoring — this is a defensible novelty, not a deviation.
- Schloegel's Sec. V-F multiple-testing guidance matters if the user reports per-project results plus a pooled result: with ~10 projects, per-project Mann-Whitney tests need a correction (or a stratified/pooled survival model, which handles it naturally).

### Gaps
- Klees et al. do not give a numeric minimum trial count anywhere; anyone citing "Klees recommends 30 trials" is citing their *experimental setup*, not a recommendation. Worth stating carefully in the paper.
- I did not verify Schloegel's exact Table/Figure numbering against the camera-ready IEEE version (I used the author arXiv version); section letters (V-A … V-F) are from that version.

## Q2. Benchmark suites: size, ground truth, target metric, venue

### Takeaway
Only Magma, LAVA-M, CGC and RevBugBench are *designed* for ground-truth bug counting; FuzzBench, UniBench/UNIFUZZ and ProFuzzBench are coverage-oriented. **ARVO is neither** — it is a reproducibility corpus, not a benchmark with a defined task or metric, which is both the user's opportunity and the thing a reviewer will poke at.

FSE084](https://doi.org/10.1145/3797112)

### Inferences
- ARVO's **defining advantage over Magma for this project** is that the bug lives in the *original, unmodified* project at a real historical commit — no forward-porting, so the Elahi & Wang critique of Magma does not apply. That is a strong, citable justification for choosing ARVO over Magma.
- ARVO's **defining disadvantage** is that it has no established evaluation protocol, so the user is defining one. That makes the *selection protocol* (Q3) the load-bearing part of the paper's defensibility, not the dataset choice.
- Because ARVO ships pre/post-patch Docker images and a known crashing input, the user's setup gets something Magma also has and FuzzBench does not: an **oracle for "is this crash the target bug"**, which removes the crash-deduplication problem Klees Sec. 7 warns about (the 57,142→9 collapse).

- ProFuzzBench's protocol count (10) is confirmed from the paper, and its README confirms each protocol may carry multiple server implementations — but I did not enumerate the full protocol → implementation mapping, so do not state a total target count. UNIFUZZ's per-program bug counts are also unconfirmed.

## Q3. PROJECT SELECTION PRECEDENT (the important one)

### Takeaway
There is **no widely-cited fuzzing paper that documents a seeded random sample of targets** — the field's norm is a *documented deterministic filter chain* ("everything that satisfies criteria X, Y, Z, and here is the count at each stage"), and reviewers accept that. The strongest, most recent precedents are **CyberGym** (three-stage QA pipeline over ARVO) and **SoK: Where to Fuzz?** (crawl-reproduce-or-drop over OSS-Fuzz). The canonical anti-cherry-picking citations are Klees' median-of-7 finding and Böhme et al.'s "no fewer than 10 programs."

### Cited Findings — 2024–2026 precedents with concrete, quotable protocols

**CyberGym (arXiv:2506.02548, ICLR 2026) — the closest precedent for "we built our set from ARVO":**
- Final set: **1,507 instances across 188 projects**; disclosure dates **2017-01-01 to 2025-04-21**.
- Sourcing: "**1,368 instances sourced from ARVO dataset (up to July 31, 2024)**" plus "**139 more recent vulnerabilities**" collected directly from OSS-Fuzz through 2025-04-21.
- Three-stage quality-assurance pipeline, stated as a protocol:
  1. **Informative-description filter** — drop instances where "the patch commit's message does not provide sufficient information about the vulnerability" or describes multiple issues; implemented with "GPT-4.1 as a judge" using "manually inspected cases as few-shot examples", reporting **96% precision** on a **300-instance validation subset**.
  2. **Reproducibility validation** — "re-run the ground truth PoC on the pre-patch and post-patch executables to ensure that the vulnerability can be reproduced."
  3. **Redundancy/dedup** — exclude "cases where multiple instances refer to the same patch commit and executables with similar logic, identified by comparing their crash stack traces."
- Task oracle: a **dual-binary check** — the generated input must crash the vulnerable binary but **not** the patched one.
— [arXiv:2506.02548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02548), [github.com/sunblaze-ucb/cybergym](https://github.com/sunblaze-ucb/cybergym)

**SoK: Where to Fuzz? Assessing Target Selection Methods in Directed Fuzzing (Weissberg et al.; arXiv:2502.08341; [mlsec.org/docs/2024c-asiaccs.pdf](https://mlsec.org/docs/2024c-asiaccs.pdf)):**
- "we assemble a dataset of more than **1,600 crashes from 97 software projects of OSS-Fuzz**, which to the best of our knowledge represents the **largest corpus of reproducible crashes to date**." Exact figure used elsewhere in the paper: "**1,621 reproducible crashes**."
- The selection is a *reproduce-or-drop* pipeline, stated as a figure caption (Fig. 3, "Dataset generation process"): "We crawl OSS-Fuzz, which yields a crashing input and a fuzzing configuration for a project (①). To reproduce the crash, we search for a commit of the project which crashes (②) when executed under the input from OSS-Fuzz (③). Once we reproduce a crash, we extract functions from the project's code …"
- Stated inclusion requirements: "For using crashing inputs identified from fuzzing for our ground truth, we require two things: (1) Crashes need to be **reproducible** to verify that they are correct, and (2) we need information about **which specific functions are involved in the crash**."
- Representativeness argument they make: OSS-Fuzz has fuzzed these projects "since 2016 and publicly discloses their findings. While there is still no guarantee that OSS-Fuzz identified every bug, **the massive amount of time spent fuzzing these targets is likely to find a large majority of crashes reachable by a fuzzer.**" — this is a reusable justification sentence.
- Bias hedging when ground truth is uncertain: they define **two matching policies**, "Optimistic matching" (NDCG↑, "overestimates the performance … serves as an upper bound") and "Pessimistic matching" (lower bound), and report both.

**Other 2024–2026 selection protocols (source is a search-engine summary; verify before quoting):**
- *Dissecting Software Graphs: Structural Insights for Driver-Guided Fuzzing* ([arXiv:2608.12859](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.12859)): starts from OSS-Fuzz's **1,325 projects** at the time of study and restricts "the benchmark space to programs implemented primarily in C or C++, resulting in **501 projects**." **UNVERIFIED** (snippet-level).
- *Fuzzing at Scale: The Untold Story of the Scheduler* ([arXiv:2406.18058](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18058)): compiles "a benchmark of around **5,000 Ubuntu programs**" (from the abstract), and observes that "evaluation programs are typically sampled from OSS-Fuzz, which contains around 1,000 target programs, with roughly half having source code in C/C++." **UNVERIFIED** for the sampling sentence (snippet-level).
- *FuzzingBrain-Bench V1* ([arXiv:2608.25158](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25158v1)): **77 challenges across 43 open-source projects** (36 C, 32 C++, 9 Java/JVM), sourced from OSS-Fuzz, issue trackers, PRs and advisories. Determinism filter, quoted: "the PoC is run against the harness binary inside the image **three times**. The challenge is kept only if **all three runs exhibit the same crash behavior**, and is discarded otherwise." Does **not** use ARVO and does **not** use random sampling.

**Random sampling that does exist in this literature — but of *papers*, not targets:**
- Schloegel et al. selected "**150 of 289** candidate fuzzing papers (**52% random selection**)" for their SoK. This is the only documented random-sample-from-a-corpus protocol I found in the fuzzing-evaluation canon, and it is a sampling of literature, not of fuzz targets. — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)

### Cited Findings — documented *exclusion* filters stated up front (the defensible pattern)

- **Böhme & Falk, FSE 2020** pre-declare an exclusion rule with the resulting count: "When reporting coverage results, we removed those programs where **more than 15% of runs crash** (leaving **13 programs** with 1.2 MLoC). As [LibFuzzer] aborts when the first crash is found, the coverage results for those subjects would be unreliable." They also pre-declare a seed policy: "we removed all initial seed corpora" because with seeds "campaigns are often for saturation". — [FSE20 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/FSE20.EmpiricalLaw.pdf)
- **Böhme & Falk** also state their per-suite configuration as a tuple, which is a clean reporting template: "OSS-Fuzz (**263 programs, 58.3 MLoC, 6 hours, 4 repetitions**)"; "FTS (**25 programs, 2.0 MLoC, 6 hours, 20 repetitions**)"; "Open-Source (**6 programs, 6.1 MLoC, 96 hours, 10 repetitions**)". — [FSE20 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/FSE20.EmpiricalLaw.pdf)

### Cited Findings — the anti-cherry-picking canon to cite as motivation

- **Klees et al., Sec. 3:** "few papers use a common, diverse benchmark suite; about 6 used CGC or LAVA-M, and **2 discussed the methodology in collecting real-world programs**, while the rest used **a few handpicked programs, with little overlap** in these choices among papers. **The median number of real-world programs used in the evaluation was 7**, and the most commonly used programs (binutils) were shared by only four papers." — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Klees et al.'s explicit cherry-picking example:** a technique "did not provide a statistically significant advantage on the image processing programs. **Had these programs been included in its evaluation, readers might have drawn more nuanced conclusions about its advantages.**" — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Klees et al. concede the problem is open:** "We do not claim that this is a complete benchmark suite; in fact, we think that deriving a good benchmark suite is an **open problem**." — [arXiv:1808.09700](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09700)
- **Schloegel et al. Sec. V-B (Targets under Test):** "Selected evaluation targets should form a **representative set**"; they "highly recommend using well-established benchmarks, such as FuzzBench"; and papers must explain any "patches applied to targets." — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Schloegel et al.'s evidence of target monoculture (Table II):** "On average, fuzzing papers evaluate on **8.9 targets**. In summary, we found **753 different targets** used across all studied papers; of these, **76% (576) were evaluated in only one paper**." Most-tested: objdump/readelf (25 uses), nm/tcpdump (20), libpng (19), libtiff (17); the set is "strongly biased towards byte-oriented file formats, especially binutils." — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Böhme, Szekeres & Metzman, ICSE 2022:** "We do **not** recommend using less than **10 benchmark programs** for coverage-based fuzzer evaluation", and "at least 10 representative programs". — [ICSE22 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE22.pdf)
- **Böhme et al. also name the failure mode the user is defending against:** researchers may "unknowingly tune their fuzzer implementation to the bugs in the benchmark", a **confirmation bias** that "invalidates some empirical evidence in a well-cited paper" (citing Zeller et al.). Their mitigation: augment bug-based evaluation with coverage-based evaluation. — [ICSE22 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE22.pdf)

### Inferences (concrete, citable recommendations for the user's paper)

1. **Write the selection as a filter cascade with a count at every stage** — this is the pattern CyberGym, Where-to-Fuzz and Böhme & Falk all use, and it is what reviewers recognize. E.g.: *ARVO N vulns → C/C++ only → project builds under our harness → baseline reproduces the crash in ≤T on ≥k/k trials → not a duplicate patch commit → distinct project cap of m → final set of P projects / B bugs*. Publish the script and the intermediate lists.
2. **Then add a seeded random sample on top of the surviving pool**, and say so in one sentence: "from the N targets surviving the filters, we drew a uniform random sample of n without replacement using seed S; the script and the drawn list are in the artifact." Precedent for randomly sampling a corpus in this literature is thin (Schloegel's 52% paper sample is the closest analogue), so **this is a strength to advertise, not a convention to appeal to** — it strictly dominates the field's handpicking norm documented by Klees (median 7, little overlap) and Schloegel (76% of targets appear in exactly one paper).
3. **Pre-declare and report the drop-outs.** Böhme & Falk's ">15% of runs crash → removed, leaving 13 programs" is the model: the exclusion rule is stated *before* the outcome, and the surviving count is given. The user's equivalents are "baseline never reproduces the bug in the budget" and "the optimized build fails to compile / fails the differential test."
4. **Report every project, including the losses.** Klees' image-processing example is the strongest argument for a per-project table with all arms, including projects where the optimized arm is slower or ties.
5. **Meet the count floor explicitly:** ≥10 distinct *projects* (Böhme ICSE'22), and note the field average of 8.9 targets (Schloegel) so a 10-project set is above the norm. If the user has ~9–10 bugs total across fewer than 10 projects, say the project count and the bug count separately and be explicit about the difference — reviewers read "targets" as programs.
6. **Cap per-project representation.** The Schloegel monoculture data (binutils dominance) gives a citable reason to allow at most one or two bugs per project, preventing one easy project from carrying the result.

## Q5. Coverage saturation and how long campaigns must run

### Takeaway
The quantitative consensus: **coverage plateaus around 12–14 hours** on typical FuzzBench-class targets, but **plateau is not saturation** — "reachable coverage" estimators all predict *false peaks*, and a 24-hour campaign is the field's floor precisely because rank reversals happen after the plateau. On bug discovery, the cost curve is brutal: **linearly more bugs costs exponentially more machines**.

### Cited Findings — plateau / stopping (2023–2026 tier first)

- **In Bugs We Trust? (Madadi, Lee, Aschermann, Böhme), *Proc. ACM Softw. Eng.* Vol. 3, FSE, Article FSE084, July 2026, [10.1145/3797112](https://doi.org/10.1145/3797112)** — the most recent word on benchmarking randomness:
  - Defines **concordance** = "mean split-half reliability, i.e., the expected agreement on the benchmark outcome between two random halves of the benchmarking suite."
  - "In our experiments with FuzzBench and Magma, we find that the concordance of **coverage-based benchmarking is consistently strong** while that of **bug-based benchmarking is weak on FuzzBench and moderate on Magma**."
  - "the split-half reliability of the ranking of fuzzers based on the **number of bugs found in 23 hours is weak**."
  - "In contrast to FuzzBench, for the Magma benchmark suite (which was designed for bug-based evaluation) a coverage-based evaluation does **not** predict the outcome of a bug-based evaluation better than an independent bug-based evaluation."
  - Green-fuzzing application: "the resources of a procedure with higher concordance can be reduced more substantially (**in terms of campaign length or benchmark sampling size**) while maintaining a similar benchmark outcome as a procedure with lower concordance", with carbon savings reported.
- **Lipp et al., "Green Fuzzing: A Saturation-based Stopping Criterion using Vulnerability Prediction", ISSTA 2023** ([mboehme.github.io/paper/ISSTA23.pdf](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ISSTA23.pdf), [10.1145/3597926.3598043](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3597926.3598043)):
  - Overall study scale: "**30 open-source C programs** with a total of **240 security bugs** and **1,280 fuzzing campaigns**."
  - Evaluation configuration: "**eight subject programs** (disjoint from the ML training set) with another **119 security bugs**, **eight state-of-the-art greybox fuzzers**, and **20 campaign repetitions** (i.e., **1,280 fuzzing campaigns**), each running for **24 hours**."
  - Headline result (**Section 5.2**): "when terminating 24-hour fuzzing campaigns based on the saturation of covered potentially vulnerable functions instead [of] triggered crashes or regular function coverage, **6–12 hours of fuzzing can be saved, while missing fewer than 0.5 out of 12.5 bugs on average**."
  - Model quality: "average ROC–AUC scores of ~0.8 (see Section 5.1)."
- **Gao, Pham et al., "Beyond the Coverage Plateau: A Comprehensive Study of Fuzz Blockers (Registered Report)"** — plateau timing, with a figure-level number:
  - "results on large benchmarks such as **FuzzBench** indicate that the state-of-the-art fuzzers often **reach a plateau after a certain period, typically around 12 hours**."
  - Fig. 1 caption (SBFT'23 Fuzzing Competition, LibPNG): "**At least 20 trials/fuzzer, 23 hours per trial. Most fuzzers reach their plateau within 14 hours.**"
  - Subjects: LibPNG, iGraph, OpenSSL — "All of these popular programs are included in the OSS-Fuzz project."
- **Shirai, Nourry, Kashiwa, Fujiwara et al., "Large-Scale Empirical Analysis of Continuous Fuzzing: Insights from 1 Million Fuzzing Sessions", IEEE TSE 2025** ([arXiv:2510.16433](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.16433)):
  - Scale: "approximately **1.12 million fuzzing sessions** from **878 projects** participating in OSS-Fuzz"; "more than **3.95 million build logs**, **1 million coverage reports**, and over **70,000 issue reports**."
  - Findings: "many fuzzing bugs are detected in the **early stages** and … coverage generally increases over time"; "fuzzing bugs are more likely [to be found when coverage fluctuates]".
  - Related-work numbers it cites: "fuzzing coverage increases **logarithmically** over time, meaning that long fuzzing sessions give diminishing return over time"; "a report suggests effective fuzzing often spans **at least one week, with 30 days considered ideal**"; "Klooster et al. showed that **short sessions of 15 minutes** can also be effective to detect critical bugs."

### Cited Findings — Liyanage et al., "Reachable Coverage: Estimating Saturation in Fuzzing", ICSE 2023

- Data scale: they plot coverage over time "for **all week-long fuzzing campaigns** in Fuzzbench that were **not** terminated prematurely. Together they involve **32 widely-used open-source programs, 11 fuzzers (incl. AFL++), and more than one CPU-century** worth of fuzzing campaigns." Default configuration, provided harnesses and initial seeds. — [ICSE23 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE23-effectiveness.pdf)
- Contribution statement: "An empirical investigation of the size of a fuzzer's search space. Particularly, we study the asymptotic behavior of the coverage achieved over time S(n) as n grows large (**7 days**). For **31 out of 32 programs** …" *(the completion of this sentence was not recoverable from my text extraction — see Gaps)*
- They evaluate "**seven state-of-the-art estimators** … and **two static analysis tools**". Result: "the **Jackknife estimators** exhibit the smallest magnitude in bias and variance compared to the others."
- Static analysis verdict (this is the OSS-Fuzz-relevant one): "For static analysis tools, we find that they **over- and under-count** reachable coverage. The static approximation is inaccurate, but it is difficult to know exactly how inaccurate the approximation is or how to improve it." OSS-Fuzz's own tool, **Fuzz-Introspector**, is the instance discussed.
- **The key caution against treating a plateau as completion:** "all estimators predict **false peaks**: **More coverage can be achieved upon reaching the predicted reachable coverage.**" Also: "existing estimators **under-estimate** fuzzer effectiveness."
- Estimators are "asymptotically consistent, meaning that their accuracy improves with campaign length."
- Framing of why the ratio matters: "The proportion S(n)/S of reachable coverage currently achieved provides a measure of progress of the ongoing fuzzing campaign towards completion (i.e., when to stop)."
— all from [ICSE23 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE23-effectiveness.pdf)

### Cited Findings — Böhme & Falk, "Fuzzing: On the Exponential Cost of Vulnerability Discovery", ESEC/FSE 2020

- The two empirical laws, verbatim from the abstract: "Given a **non-deterministic** fuzzer, finding the **same bugs linearly faster requires linearly more machines**. For instance, with twice the machines, we can find all known bugs in half the time. Yet, finding **linearly more bugs in the same time requires exponentially more machines**. For instance, for every new bug we want to find in 24 hours, we might need **twice more machines**. Similarly for coverage. With exponentially more machines, we can cover the **same code exponentially faster**, but **uncovered code only linearly faster**. In other words, **re-discovering the same vulnerabilities is cheap but finding new vulnerabilities is expensive**. This holds even under the simplifying assumption of **no** parallelization overhead."
- Scale: "over **four CPU years** worth of fuzzing campaigns involving **almost three hundred open source programs**, **two** state-of-the-art greybox fuzzers, **four** measures of code coverage, and **two** measures of vulnerability discovery."
- Their illustrative extrapolation: an attacker with "**2× more (5 million) machines** finds an unknown critical bug with ≈**15% likelihood or less** while the attacker with **100× more (250 million!) machines** only finds **five (5) unknown critical bugs or less**."
- Subject configurations (a good reporting template): OSS-Fuzz (**263 programs, 58.3 MLoC, 6 hours, 4 repetitions**); FTS (**25 programs, 2.0 MLoC, 6 hours, 20 repetitions**); Open-Source (**6 programs, 6.1 MLoC, 96 hours, 10 repetitions**).
— all from [FSE20 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/FSE20.EmpiricalLaw.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The user's design sits on the favorable side of Böhme & Falk's law.** Their experiment measures *time to re-find a known bug*, which is the **linear-cost regime** ("finding the same bugs linearly faster requires linearly more machines" / more execs). A throughput multiplier of k should translate to roughly a **1/k reduction in time-to-bug**, which is a falsifiable, pre-registerable prediction and is exactly the right citation to justify why throughput optimization is worth measuring at all. The paper should state this explicitly — it converts an FSE'20 "pessimism" result into the user's theoretical justification.
- **Trial duration:** 24 h per trial is the defensible floor (Klees Sec. 6; Schloegel V-D). The 12–14 h plateau evidence (fuzz-blockers registered report; Green Fuzzing's 6–12 h saving) means a 24 h cap is *generous* for coverage but is the right budget for a time-to-bug endpoint with censoring, since some ARVO bugs will take >12 h. If compute is tight, Green Fuzzing is the citation for stopping early, but it costs ~0.5/12.5 bugs.
- **Censoring is expected, not a defect.** Liyanage's "false peaks" result and the Klees 13 h→24 h reversal both argue that a fixed horizon *must* produce right-censored observations; the survival-analysis framing is the statistically correct response and should be presented as such.
- **Concordance (FSE 2026) is the strongest available argument for the user's arm design**: bug-based rankings are the *noisy* ones, so a bug-based comparison needs either more trials or a paired/stratified design. Pairing baseline and optimized arms on the *same* ARVO bug (same target, same seed corpus, same harness) removes the between-target variance that drives the weak split-half reliability — worth saying in the threats-to-validity section.

## Q6. Comparing two *binaries* rather than two *fuzzers*: the measurement problem

### Takeaway
There is **no paper I found that directly addresses "how do you compare coverage between two builds of the same program whose source differs."** The nearest guidance is Schloegel's rule that the *same notion of coverage* must be used and that the collection mechanism must be stated; the nearest prior art on "the program under test is not the original program" is the **forward-porting critique of Magma**. The practical answer the literature supports is: **do not compare raw coverage counts across differing binaries — compare a bug-based endpoint (time-to-bug), and use coverage only on an intersected, source-level, name-stable unit.**

### Cited Findings
- **Schloegel et al. Sec. V-E, the closest thing to a rule:** "the evaluation must ensure that the **same notion of coverage** is used for each of the compared fuzzers"; and "it is necessary to specify details such as **how coverage is collected**, for example, whether it is measured on a **non-instrumented binary**, translated blocks from an emulator, or using established means such as **lcov**." They also warn against "bitmaps with collisions". — [arXiv:2405.10220](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10220)
- **Böhme et al. ICSE'22 give the reason coverage alone cannot settle a two-binary comparison:** strong correlation between coverage and bugs, but only moderate rank agreement — "the fuzzer best at achieving coverage may be the worst at finding bugs" (restated by Madadi et al.). Their prescription is to report **both** coverage and bugs. — [ICSE22 PDF](https://mboehme.github.io/paper/ICSE22.pdf); [concordance paper](https://nimgnoeseel.github.io/assets/pdf/concordance-paper.pdf)
- **Forward-porting is the existing literature on "the binary under test is not the original binary"** and its findings transfer directly: forward-porting "is error-prone and may fail due to factors such as **architectural changes** in the software library, such as removal of specific modules, changes in the program structure, and newly added functionalities"; "future code changes may render a forward-ported bug **obsolete** or make its **trigger conditions unsatisfiable**"; "without verification, forward-porting may inject bugs which **cannot be triggered**." — Elahi & Wang, *Information Sciences* 2024, [10.1016/j.ins.2024.120142](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1016/j.ins.2024.120142) *(search-summary rendering; verify before quoting)*
- **Different coverage metrics are not interchangeable even on the same program:** "different coverage metrics often result in finding different sets of bugs, and at different times of the whole fuzzing process, the best performer may vary"; "binary coverage grows even when branch coverage does not." — Wang et al., "Analyzing Impact of Coverage Metrics in Greybox Fuzzing", RAID 2019, [usenix.org/system/files/raid2019-wang-jinghan.pdf](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/raid2019-wang-jinghan.pdf) *(search-summary rendering)*
- **Instrumentation is itself a throughput confound of the same order as the effect the user is measuring:** "AFL-QEMU, AFL-Dyninst, and AFL-Clang incur overheads of **612%, 518%, and 36%**, respectively"; coverage instrumentation imposes "an average overhead of **1.46×**." — Nagy & Hicks, "Full-speed Fuzzing", IEEE S&P 2019, [arXiv:1812.11875](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11875) *(search-summary rendering of the paper's numbers)*
- **CyberGym's dual-binary oracle** is the cleanest published pattern for making a claim about one binary robust: the PoC "must crash the vulnerable binary but **not** the patched one." — [arXiv:2506.02548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02548)

### Inferences (recommendations the user can act on)
1. **Make time-to-bug, not coverage, the primary endpoint** — it is the only metric that is definitionally comparable across two binaries, because the bug identity is fixed by ARVO's reproducer + patch pair. Cite Klees Sec. 7 (ground truth over heuristics) and Böhme ICSE'22 (report both, but bugs are the endpoint that matters).
2. **If coverage is reported at all, report it on a source-level, name-stable unit intersected across the two builds** — e.g. `lcov`/clang source-based *function* or *line* coverage restricted to functions present and unmodified in both variants. Edge/basic-block/bitmap counts are meaningless across builds because the CFG changed by construction. Schloegel V-E is the citation for "state exactly how coverage was collected."
3. **Measure coverage on a separate, identically-built coverage binary, not on the fuzzing binary.** Both arms' coverage binaries should be compiled with the *same* coverage instrumentation and the *same* optimization level, differing only in the source edit under test, so the instrumentation overhead cancels. (This mirrors FuzzBench's practice of a dedicated coverage build — **UNVERIFIED in this pass**, worth confirming in the FuzzBench paper/docs before citing.)
4. **Report execs/sec and total execs alongside wall-clock time-to-bug.** This decomposes the effect into "the optimization made each exec cheaper" vs "the optimization changed the search", which is the question a reviewer will ask. Böhme & Falk's linear law is the yardstick: if time-to-bug does *not* drop by ~1/k when execs/sec rises by k, something other than throughput changed.
5. **Add a semantic-equivalence gate and report it**, analogous to CyberGym's dual-binary check: the optimized binary must still crash on the ARVO reproducer input and must produce identical outputs to baseline on the corpus. Without it, a reviewer's first objection is "you made it faster by making it do less." This is the direct analogue of the forward-porting critique, and it is the single highest-value experimental safeguard.
6. **State the anti-instrumentation-confound argument explicitly:** both arms run the identical fuzzer, identical sanitizer set, identical `-O` level, identical harness, identical seed corpus, identical hardware pinning, and the only difference is the project source edit. Schloegel V-D's hardware/cores/runtime disclosure list is the checklist to satisfy.

### Gaps
- **No prior work found that specifically formalizes coverage comparison between two source-divergent builds of the same program.** This appears to be a genuine open measurement problem; the user may want to frame it as such (and Klees' own "deriving a good benchmark suite is an open problem" is precedent for admitting an open problem in an evaluation section).
- Whether FuzzBench uses a dedicated uninstrumented/clang-source-based coverage binary is **UNVERIFIED** here — widely believed, not checked in this pass.
- The Nagy & Hicks, Wang et al., and Elahi & Wang quotes are search-summary renderings, not text I extracted from the PDFs. Verify before quoting verbatim.
